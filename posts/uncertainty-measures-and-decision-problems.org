While working on my latest paper on designing experiments for external decision
makers (which should be up on arXiv soon), I went down a rabbit hole in the
literature on measures of uncertainty. Along the way I came across some
interesting old papers that I had either never seen or never bothered to read.
Together, they point to one idea: that specifying a reasonable measure of
uncertainty is the same thing as specifying a decision problem. This was not
obvious to me beforehand, and I suspect it is not obvious to most people who use
these measures. This post is my attempt to document this idea for my future
self, and for the odd reader with an interest in decision theory or uncertainty
quantification.

** Decision Theory 101

I will start with a brief review of the setup of Bayesian decision theory
[cite:@savage1954foundations]. Let \(z \in \mathcal{Z}\) denote the hidden state
of the world, and let \(a \in \mathcal{A}\) denote an action available to us.
The state is assumed to carry all information that is relevant for the decision,
so that each pair of a state and an action leads to a definite consequence for
the decision maker (us). The table below gives an example of a decision problem.

#+CAPTION: Savage's classic example of a decision problem about making an omelette, which illustrates acts, states, and consequences [cite:@savage1954foundations, Table 1]. We start with five good eggs already broken into a bowl, and we need to decide what to do with a sixth egg, which may or may not be rotten.
#+NAME: tab:omelette
| Action            | State: Good                                   | State: Rotten                             |
|-------------------+-----------------------------------------------+-------------------------------------------|
| break into bowl   | six-egg omelette                              | no omelette, and five good eggs destroyed |
| break into saucer | six-egg omelette, and a saucer to wash        | five-egg omelette, and a saucer to wash   |
| throw away        | five-egg omelette, and one good egg destroyed | five-egg omelette                         |

Savage's theorem says that if our preferences between actions satisfy a handful
of axioms, which amount to requiring that we are consistent in what we prefer,
then there exist a probability distribution \(\mu\) over \(\mathcal{Z}\) and a
loss function \(\ell: \mathcal{A} \times \mathcal{Z} \to \mathbb{R}\) such that
we prefer one action to another exactly when it has lower expected loss. The
distribution \(\mu\) represents our beliefs about the hidden state, the loss
represents our preferences over consequences, and the rational action is the one
that minimises expected loss:
\[
    a^{\star} \in \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mu}[\ell(a, z)].
\]

** Measures of Uncertainty

Let us now see how a decision problem naturally gives rise to a measure of
uncertainty. For a fixed decision problem and a belief \(\mu\) over
\(\mathcal{Z}\), consider the quantity
\[
    \mathbb{H}_{\ell}[\mu] \coloneqq \inf_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mu}[\ell(a, z)] - \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mu}\left[\inf_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \ell(a, z)\right].
\]
The first term is the best expected loss we can achieve without knowing the
state, and the second is the expected loss we would achieve if we were told the
state before acting. The difference is therefore the expected cost of our
uncertainty about \(z\). [cite/t:@raiffa1961applied] called it the expected
value of perfect information (EVPI): it is the most we should be willing to pay
an oracle who will reveal \(z\). Note that, once the decision problem is fixed,
\(\mathbb{H}_{\ell}\) depends on the belief \(\mu\) alone; it takes a
distribution and returns a number that says how much our ignorance of the state
costs us.

If the loss is normalised so that the best action in every state has zero loss,
i.e. \(\inf_{a} \ell(a, z) = 0\) for all \(z\), the second term vanishes and the
EVPI reduces to the Bayes risk,
\[
    \mathbb{H}_{\ell}[\mu] = \inf_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mu}[\ell(a, z)].
\]
This is what [cite/t:@grunwald2004game] call a generalised entropy. The Bayes
risk makes sense even when the normalisation does not, for instance when
\(\inf_{a} \ell(a, z) = -\infty\), and I will use it as the definition of
\(\mathbb{H}_{\ell}\) from here on.

The property that makes \(\mathbb{H}_{\ell}\) interesting is the following.
Suppose we hold a belief \(\mu\), observe data \(Y\), and update to the
posterior \(\mu_{Y}\). Averaged over the data, the posterior returns the prior:
\(\mathbb{E}_{Y}[\mu_{Y}] = \mu\), where the expectation is under the marginal
distribution of \(Y\) implied by \(\mu\) and the likelihood. Under mild
conditions (all of which hold if \(\ell\) is bounded below, for
example),[fn:integrability] we then have
\[
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbb{E}_{Y}\bigl[\mathbb{H}_{\ell}[\mu_{Y}]\bigr]
    & = \mathbb{E}_{Y}\left[\inf_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mu_{Y}}[\ell(a, z)]\right] \\
    & \leq \inf_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}_{Y} \left[\mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mu_{Y}}[\ell(a, z)]\right] \\
    & = \inf_{a \in \mathcal{A}} \mathbb{E}_{z \sim \mu}[\ell(a, z)]
      = \mathbb{H}_{\ell}[\mu].
\end{aligned}
\]
In words: on average, new information cannot increase H.  In terms of the EVPI,
the oracle is worth less, on average, once we have seen some data, because part
of its job has already been done.

** The Equivalence

This property is so natural that it is tempting to turn the argument around and
take it as the /definition/ of a measure of uncertainty: a function of the
belief that cannot increase on average under any experiment. This is close to
what [cite/t:@degroot1962uncertainty] did. Generalising
[cite/t:@lindley1956measure], who had proved the property for Shannon entropy,
DeGroot allowed any non-negative function of the belief to serve as an
uncertainty measure, required that no experiment increase it on average, and
characterised the functions that satisfy this property. Common measures such as
Shannon entropy and variance qualify, and, as we have just seen, so does the
generalised entropy of every decision problem.[fn:normalisations]

The definition is also more than a sanity check, because it pins down the class
of measures almost completely. First, it forces concavity. Take any two beliefs
\(\mu_{0}\) and \(\mu_{1}\), let \(\mu = \lambda \mu_{1} + (1 - \lambda)
\mu_{0}\), and consider the experiment that reports \(Y = 1\) with probability
\(\lambda\) and \(Y = 0\) otherwise, with posteriors \(\mu_{1}\) and
\(\mu_{0}\). Then the defining property reads \(\mathbb{H}[\mu] \geq \lambda
\mathbb{H}[\mu_{1}] + (1 - \lambda) \mathbb{H}[\mu_{0}]\), which is concavity.
Second, on a finite state space the converse holds: a concave function of the
belief satisfies the defining property, by Jensen's inequality applied to the
random posterior, since a belief on \(n\) states is a point in the simplex in
\(\mathbb{R}^{n}\) [cite:@degroot1962uncertainty, Theorem 2.1]. Third, and this
is the step that closes the loop, every non-negative concave function on the
simplex is the Bayes risk of some decision problem
[cite:@degroot1962uncertainty; @bect2026uncertainty].
Put together: on a finite state space, a measure of uncertainty, a concave
function of the belief, and the Bayes risk of a decision problem are the same
thing.

DeGroot appears to have been the first to draw this conclusion, writing on page
408 that "the distinction sometimes made, as in [cite/t:@lindley1956measure],
between decision problems and problems in which the experimenter simply wants to
gain knowledge may not be very sharp." He refers to Lindley there because
Lindley had proposed the expected information gain (EIG), the expected reduction
in Shannon entropy from prior to posterior, precisely as a criterion for the
experimenter who has no action in mind and simply wants to "gain knowledge about
the state of nature" [cite:@lindley1956measure, page 987]. The explicit decision
problem behind the EIG was later written down by [cite/t:@bernardo1979expected]:
take \(\mathcal{A}\) to be the set of distributions over \(\mathcal{Z}\) and
\(\ell(a, z) = -\log a(z)\), so that the generalised entropy is Shannon entropy
and the EIG is the expected reduction in Bayes risk. Bernardo went further and
argued that the log loss is the right loss for a pure inference problem,
which is the reason the EIG is still the default in Bayesian experimental
design.

** Caveats and Open Problems

- The defining property says that \(\mathbb{H}\) satisfies Jensen's inequality
  for random posteriors, and on a finite space this is equivalent to concavity.
  On infinite spaces concavity remains necessary but is no longer sufficient,
  and [cite/t:@bect2026uncertainty, Theorem 5] provide a counterexample.
- All Bayes risks define valid uncertainty measures, but it is not known whether all
  valid uncertainty measures can be expressed as Bayes risks for some decision
  problem. This is Open Problem 2 in [cite/t:@bect2026uncertainty]. On finite
  spaces the answer is yes [cite:@bect2026uncertainty, Theorem 6].

** Two Literatures

Something I noticed while researching this post is that the problem of measuring
uncertainty and information is studied in both the statistics and economics
literature, and the two have almost completely diverged from each other. Both
[cite/t:@frankel2019quantifying] and [cite/t:@bect2026uncertainty] study
uncertainty measures, and arrive at essentially the same definition, but the
only reference they have in common is the review of proper scoring rules by
[cite/t:@gneiting2007strictly]. More broadly, the one shared ancestor of the two
literatures seems to be [cite/t:@blackwell1951comparison;
@blackwell1953equivalent]. While working on my paper, I would have missed the
extremely relevant work of [cite/t:@kamenica2011bayesian] had Claude not found
it while I was searching for related work, and the statistics literature is
oblivious to it.

** Conclusion

The practical upshot is a question worth asking whenever an uncertainty measure
appears in a method: which decision problem is it the Bayes risk of, and is that
the problem we actually care about? For entropy the answer is the log loss over
predictive distributions, which is a good choice for pure inference and a
strange one for almost anything else. The measure is never neutral; it always
encodes a loss, whether or not we wrote one down.
