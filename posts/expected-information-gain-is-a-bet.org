The expected information gain [cite:EIG, @lindley1956measure], the expected
reduction in Shannon (or differential) entropy from the prior to the posterior,
is a popular objective in Bayesian experimental design. It has several useful
interpretations: it is the mutual information between the data and the latent
parameters of interest \(z\), and it is the expected reduction in Bayes risk for
a decision problem under the logarithmic score (see my [[./expected-information-as-expected-utility.html][earlier post on expected
information as expected utility]]). This post is about yet another
interpretation: the EIG is the optimal growth rate of a bettor who bets against
the null hypothesis that the data are uninformative about \(z\).

I begin by reviewing Kelly's result on betting with side information and its
connection to the more general testing-by-betting paradigm, and then show that
the EIG is exactly Kelly's growth rate when the side information is \(z\).

** A Brief Review of Kelly Betting

[cite/t:@kelly1956new] considered a gambler with access to side information.
Suppose that in each round a pair \((Y_{i}, Z_{i})\) is drawn i.i.d. from a
joint distribution \(q(y, z)\), with \(Y_{i}\) taking values in a finite set
\(\mathcal{Y}\). The bookmaker knows only the marginal law \(q(y)\) of the
outcome and offers fair odds accordingly: a unit stake on outcome \(y\) returns
\(1/q(y)\) if \(Y_{i} = y\) and nothing otherwise. We, however, receive
\(Z_{i}\) over a "private wire" before each round. We start with an initial
wealth \(V_{0}\), and in each round we observe \(Z_{i} = z\) and stake a
fraction \(b(y \mid z)\) of our current wealth on each \(y \in \mathcal{Y}\).
Kelly asked which strategy \(b\) maximises the logarithmic rate of
growth[fn:log] of our wealth,
\[
    G(b) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \log \frac{V_{N}}{V_{0}},
\]
where \(V_{N}\) is our wealth after round \(N\). Throughout, \(\log\) is the
natural logarithm; Kelly used base 2, which only changes the units from nats to
bits. By the law of large numbers, with probability one we have
\[
    G(b) = \mathbb{E}\left[\log \frac{b(Y \mid Z)}{q(Y)}\right]
    = \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\Big[\operatorname{KL}[q(\cdot \mid z) \,\Vert\, q]
      - \operatorname{KL}[q(\cdot \mid z) \,\Vert\, b(\cdot \mid z)]\Big].
\]
Since the KL divergence is non-negative and zero only when its arguments
coincide, Kelly's result follows immediately: the optimal strategy and the
resulting optimal rate of growth are
\[
    b^{\star}(y \mid z) = q(y \mid z),
    \qquad
    G(b^{\star})
    = \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\left[\operatorname{KL}[q(\cdot \mid z) \,\Vert\, q]\right]
    = I(Y; Z).
\]
Here \(I(Y; Z)\) is the mutual information between \(Y\) and \(Z\), defined by
the middle expression: the average KL divergence of the conditional law of
\(Y\) given \(Z\) from its marginal law, or equivalently
\(\operatorname{KL}[q(y, z) \,\Vert\, q(y) \, q(z)]\). In words, we should bet
on each outcome in proportion to our belief in it given what the wire tells us,
and our wealth then grows at a rate equal to the mutual information between the
wire and the outcome. If the wire carries no
information (\(Z\) independent of \(Y\)), the best we can do is break even.

** Testing by Betting

Kelly's formulation extends naturally to statistical testing, an idea known as
/testing by betting/ [cite:@shafer2019game; @shafer2021testing]. To see how,
suppose the wire always carries the same message \(z\), so that the outcomes
are i.i.d. draws from \(q(\cdot \mid z)\), while the bookmaker still prices
them at \(q\). Betting against this bookmaker is then a test of the null
hypothesis \(H_{0}\colon Y_{i} \sim q\) against the alternative
\(H_{1}\colon Y_{i} \sim q(\cdot \mid z)\): the bookmaker believes the null,
and we measure the evidence against it by how much money we make.

If we follow Kelly's strategy and bet \(b^{\star}(\cdot \mid z) = q(\cdot \mid
z)\), then in round \(s\) our wealth is multiplied by
\[
    E_{s} = \frac{q(y_{s} \mid z)}{q(y_{s})},
\]
so starting from \(V_{0} = 1\), our wealth after \(t\) rounds is
\[
    M_{t} = \prod_{s=1}^{t} E_{s}
    = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \frac{q(y_{s} \mid z)}{q(y_{s})},
\]
which is simply the likelihood ratio of the data. Since the odds are fair under
the null, we cannot expect to make money if the null is true:
\[
    \mathbb{E}_{q}[E_{s}] = 1
    \qquad \text{and} \qquad
    \mathbb{E}_{q}[M_{t+1} \mid Y_{1:t}] = M_{t}.
\]
That is, \(M_{t}\) is a non-negative martingale under \(H_{0}\) with
\(M_{0} = 1\), known as a /test martingale/. By Ville's inequality, for any
\(\alpha \in (0, 1)\),
\[
    \mathbb{P}_{q}\left(\exists\, t \colon M_{t} \geq 1/\alpha\right) \leq \alpha,
\]
so rejecting \(H_{0}\) as soon as our wealth reaches \(1/\alpha\) is a
level-\(\alpha\) test.

Each \(E_{s}\) is an /e-variable/, a non-negative statistic with expectation at
most \(1\) under the null, and \(M_{t}\) is an /e-process/, a non-negative
process satisfying \(\mathbb{E}_{q}[M_{\tau}] \leq 1\) for every stopping time
\(\tau\) [cite:@ramdas2025hypothesis]. Kelly's theorem tells us which bet to
make when the alternative is true. If \(Y_{i} \sim q(\cdot \mid z)\), then
\(\frac{1}{t} \log M_{t} \to \operatorname{KL}[q(\cdot \mid z) \,\Vert\, q]\)
almost surely, so our wealth grows exponentially and we eventually reject the
null. Moreover, no other e-variable for this null grows faster.
[cite/t:@grunwald2024safe] call this the growth-rate optimal (GRO) criterion.
For a simple null and a simple alternative, the GRO e-variable is exactly the
likelihood ratio \(q(\cdot \mid z)/q\), and its growth rate is
\(\operatorname{KL}[q(\cdot \mid z) \,\Vert\, q]\), the term inside the
expectation in Kelly's growth rate.

** The Experimental Design Setting

Let \(z\) be a latent variable with prior \(p(z)\). At each step
\(t \in \{1, \dots, T\}\), the experimenter chooses a design \(x_{t}\) according
to a policy, \(x_{t} \sim \pi(\cdot \mid h_{t-1})\), and observes an outcome
\(y_{t} \sim p(\cdot \mid z, x_{t}, h_{t-1})\), where \(h_{t} = ((x_{1}, y_{1}),
\dots, (x_{t}, y_{t}))\) is the history so far and \(h_{0} = \emptyset\). Since
the policy depends only on the history and not on \(z\), its terms cancel in
Bayes' rule, and the posterior is
\[
    p(z \mid h_{t}) \propto p(z) \prod_{s=1}^{t} p(y_{s} \mid z, x_{s}, h_{s-1}).
\]
The posterior predictive of the next outcome is
\[
    p(y_{t} \mid x_{t}, h_{t-1})
    = \int p(y_{t} \mid z, x_{t}, h_{t-1}) \, p(z \mid h_{t-1}) \, \mathrm{d} z.
\]

Now, for each \(z\), let
\[
    E_{t}(z) \coloneqq \frac{p(z \mid h_{t})}{p(z)} \tag{1}
\]
be the ratio of posterior to prior, so that \(E_{0}(z) = 1\). This ratio has
an interpretation as the wealth process of an oracle who knows the true
parameter \(z\). To see this, we apply Bayes' rule repeatedly and factorise the
above ratio as
\[
    E_{t}(z) = \prod_{s=1}^{t}
    \frac{p(y_{s} \mid z, x_{s}, h_{s-1})}{p(y_{s} \mid x_{s}, h_{s-1})}. \tag{2}
\]
Each factor in the above equation has a betting interpretation. Before \(y_{s}\)
is revealed, a bookmaker who believes the model but does not know \(z\) offers
fair odds according to the posterior predictive \(p(\cdot \mid x_{s},
h_{s-1})\). A bettor who knows \(z\) stakes her whole wealth according to
\(p(\cdot \mid z, x_{s}, h_{s-1})\), which, by the same argument as Kelly's
theorem applied to a single round, is the growth-optimal bet given her
information. Her wealth is then multiplied by the \(s\)-th factor in (2), so
\(E_{t}(z)\) is her wealth after \(t\) design steps, whichever policy chose the
designs.

** The Uninformative Null and the EIG

We can now treat one entire experiment as a single round of Kelly's game.
Nature draws \(z \sim p(z)\), which the oracle receives over the private wire,
and the outcome she bets on is the whole history \(h_{T}\). The bookmaker, who
does not know \(z\), prices this outcome at its marginal law \(p(h_{T})\) under
the policy \(\pi\); equivalently, by (2), the bookmaker quotes the posterior
predictive odds one design step at a time, and the oracle places her bet one
step at a time too.

Fix \(z\). The natural null hypothesis is that /the experiment is
uninformative/, that is, the data carry no trace of \(z\):
\(H_{0}\colon h_{T} \sim p(h_{T})\), against the alternative
\(H_{1}\colon h_{T} \sim p(h_{T} \mid z)\). As before, the policy's terms
cancel in the likelihood ratio,
\[
    \frac{p(h_{T} \mid z)}{p(h_{T})}
    = \frac{p(z \mid h_{T})}{p(z)}
    = E_{T}(z), \tag{3}
\]
so the oracle's wealth is exactly the likelihood ratio of the alternative
against the null, just as \(M_{t}\) was in the testing-by-betting section, and
hence the GRO e-variable for this test. It is straightforward to show that,
under the null, \((E_{t}(z))_{0 \leq t \leq T}\) is a non-negative martingale
with \(E_{0}(z) = 1\), so the oracle's wealth is a test martingale for every
\(z\). In betting terms, the bookmaker's odds are fair under the null, so even
the oracle cannot expect to make money if \(z\) leaves no trace in the data.
The optimal growth rate of the test is
\(\operatorname{KL}[p(h_{T} \mid z) \,\Vert\, p(h_{T})]\).

Averaging over the message on the wire, Kelly's theorem gives the oracle's
growth rate across repeated experiments as
\[
    \mathbb{E}_{p(z)}\left[\operatorname{KL}[p(h_{T} \mid z) \,\Vert\, p(h_{T})]\right]
    = I(z; h_{T})
    = \operatorname{EIG}_{T}(\pi). \tag{4}
\]
That is, the EIG is exactly Kelly's growth rate for a gambler whose private
wire carries \(z\), betting on the outcome of the experiment against a
bookmaker who believes it is uninformative about \(z\).

** Conclusion

Seen this way, experimental design is the problem of choosing which game to
play. The bookmaker always offers fair odds under the model, so no experiment
lets a bettor without knowledge of \(z\) make money. What the policy \(\pi\)
controls is how much the private wire is worth: an informative experiment is
one in which knowing \(z\) lets the oracle beat the bookmaker quickly, and the
EIG-optimal policy is the one that makes this edge as large as possible. In
testing terms, maximising the EIG chooses the experiment that, on average over
the prior, gathers evidence fastest against the hypothesis that we learn
nothing about \(z\).

[fn:log] For a discussion of why we use the logarithmic rate of growth rather
than some other measure of growth, see Section 2.2.1 in
[cite/t:@shafer2021testing].
